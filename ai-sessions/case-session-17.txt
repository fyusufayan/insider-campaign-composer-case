      344  **Reasoning.** (1) Every other mutation is version-guarded; a 
           client retry after a lost `202` must still learn "already sent

           rule; consent revocation is the stronger signal. (5) Persistin
           g a malformed destination would make every retry fail permanen
           tly at the provider and hide the real cause. (6) Parking rows 
           would leave campaigns `sending` forever under the default mock
            faults; bounded fixed retries keep liveness (G6) and are repl
           aced, not extended, by CP17's backoff.
      345  
      346  **Trade-offs.** No 100 req/s guarantee and no 429 channel paus
           e yet (the pool size bounds concurrency only); retries are not
            jittered. The consent window is bounded by "no I/O between pr
           echeck and request" but not yet by the 20 ms gate. A repeat se
           nd returns the current campaign, which may be newer than the c

  2. Rate limit

    1.05 s window, served first-in first-out so no row waits forever. SMS and

  - Request deadline: each request either leaves within 20 ms of getting its
    slot or is not sent. The last check happens immediately before the HTTP
    call; a missed slot is wasted, never refunded.
  - Guarantee: at most 100 requests per channel reach the provider in any

    never fired, and provider arrivals equalled recorded attempts. With two
    workers and a mid-send handover at N=20, the maximum was 20.

  - Permanent 4xx errors are never retried. 429s have their own budget of 10.
  - The random source is injectable for tests. The next attempt time is stored
    in PostgreSQL, so retries survive a restart and are never sent early.
  - DELIVERY_RETRY_DELAY is removed; setting it now stops the worker at startup
    with an error.

  4. 429 handling

  - A 429 pauses only its own channel, right away in memory.
  - The pause is also written to the new channel_pauses table, which survives a
    worker restart. Claiming skips a paused channel.
  - Retry-After values are bounded to 1–30 s; malformed values become 1 s, and
    values too large to parse become 30 s.
  - Each 429 is counted once.

  5. Cancellation

  POST /cancel {expected_version}:
  - Already cancelled or finished: returns 409 invalid_state whatever version is
    sent, so a repeat learns why.
  - Before sending: the approval is revoked and its frozen rows are deleted.
  - While sending: queued rows are cancelled immediately. Rows already being
    sent finish and are recorded truthfully; accepted sends stay sent.
  - The response's in_flight count is the honest upper bound on requests that
    may still reach the provider, at most one per row.

  I also fixed an existing bug: rows released or recovered after a cancel used
  to go back to pending and could never finish. They now end cancelled.

  6. Expiry sweeper

  It runs every 30 s in every worker and expires each due pending or approved
  approval exactly once. It never touches approvals already used for sending. A
  test covers the case where the sweeper starts waiting on the campaign lock
  before expiry and gets it after; the approval is still expired.

  7. Recovery

  Re-checked after the changes: a crashed worker's rows are recovered, stale
  workers can't overwrite newer results, no database transaction is open during
  a provider call (tested from inside the provider), and no key was accepted
  twice anywhere.

  I also fixed a rare case. A pre-send check could time out on our side but
  commit late; the row was then reported as a lost lease and later got a fake
  "unknown" attempt. It is now released without counting an attempt.

⏺ The review found one real leadership-fencing bug, and I fixed it. Nothing is

  The bug and the fix

  Bug: the final check before each provider request only checked the slot
  deadline. It did not check whether dispatching had stopped (shutdown or lost
  lock), and it did not check whether this process still held the dispatch lock.
  The quiet-period argument assumed the old leader notices a lost lock within
  check interval + timeout (2 s). That only holds if its watcher goroutine runs
  on time. A leader stalled right after losing its lock (GC pause, descheduling,
  SIGSTOP) could wake up, grant a slot, pass the check and send while the new
  leader was already sending.

  Fix, in delivery/gate.go and worker.go: the final check, immediately before
  the HTTP request, now also requires:
  - that dispatching hasn't been stopped, and
  - now ≤ leaseOK + interval + timeout, where leaseOK is the monotonic clock
    reading taken just before the latest successful statement on the lock
    session.

  So the old leader's last possible request is bounded by the clock, not by when
  the watcher runs.

  Answers to your six questions

  1. Two processes sending at the same time during handover? They cannot start
     requests within one rate window of each other. The old leader issues
     nothing after L + 2 s (L = lock loss). The new leader issues nothing before
     L + 2 s + W, where W = 1.05 s is the rate window. A request the old leader
     started earlier may still be in flight while the new leader starts; that
     affects arrivals only (see 4).
  2. Can a goroutine keep dispatching after the lock is lost? Goroutines waiting
     for a slot stop when the watcher notices. A goroutine already holding a
     slot is now stopped at the final check by the fence and the
     stopped-dispatch check. Before the fix it could send up to 20 ms after its
     slot, or later if the process had stalled. Requests already handed to the
     HTTP client finish.
  3. Does the quiet period cover the old leader's requests? It covers every
     request the old leader started, since those are bounded at the final check.
     It cannot cover how long such a request then takes to reach the provider.
  4. Old leader loses the database but keeps its provider connection? It can't
     run the pre-send check or confirm its lock, so it starts nothing new: the
     fence lapses within 2 s. A request it already sent may still be delivered
     late, up to the 5 s request timeout (for example after TCP retransmission).
     That case is assumed, not proven.
  5. Does the final check reject stale leadership or a cancelled context? Yes,
     since this fix. A campaign cancellation is different: the final check can't
     see a database change without I/O, so the fence for cancellation is each
     attempt's pre-send check (see section 4).
  6. Is the limit enforced at send time or only at slot time? Both, at the
     moment of the final check, not at the provider.

  1. The actual rate-limit guarantee

  - Enforced by the code: slots per process satisfy g₍ᵢ₊N₎ − gᵢ ≥ W. Every
    request passes the final check within G = 20 ms of its slot, and the leader
    fence separates processes by at least W. So at most 100 requests per channel
    per rolling second leave across the whole deployment, measured at the final
    check. This assumes only that each host's monotonic clock runs at the real
    rate. It assumes nothing about scheduling or clock offsets between hosts.
  - Assumed, not proven: that at most 100 per second arrive at the provider.
    That needs the time between the final check and arrival (connection reuse,
    kernel, network, a stalled TCP send) to stay within 30 ms. Provider-side
    tests measured it but cannot prove it. I don't claim an unconditional global
    100 req/s bound.

  2. Leadership handover

  New test TestHandoverAfterLockSessionLoss terminates the leader's lock session
  on the server mid-send:

  - the provider saw at most 20 requests per second per channel,
  - every key was accepted once.

  It passed 3 times in a row. Either worker may take the lock next; the old
  leader re-acquiring it is legitimate, since it also waits the quiet period.

  3. Channel pauses

  - A 429 pauses only its own channel; SMS and email are independent. Tests
    passed before this review.
  - Pauses only ever extend. GREATEST in SQL plus the limiter's extend-only
    pause. New test TestDurablePauseNeverShortened: a 20 s pause survives a
    later 1 s 429.
  - New leader respects the stored pause. It loads the pauses before its first
    send. If that read fails, it now gives the lock up instead of sending
    (TestPauseReadFailureFailsClosed).
  - Pauses recorded elsewhere. They are re-read after every successful lock
    check (about 1 s). This covers a former leader's late 429
    (TestDurablePauseFromElsewhereIsRespected).
  - Failed persistence. The in-memory pause applies at once. If the transaction
    that records the pause fails, the pause is written again on its own. If that
    also fails, it holds in memory only and is logged as an error; a restart
    during the pause could then resume the channel early. That is the residual
    risk of a database outage, and it is documented.
  - Retry-After parsing: values are bounded to 1–30 s. Malformed values become 1
    s; values too large to parse become 30 s.

  4. Cancellation

  - Atomic: campaign status, approval and delivery rows change in one
    transaction that locks the campaign row first.
  - The fence is each attempt's pre-send check, not the final check. A pre-send
    check that committed just before the cancel can still lead to one request
    within 20 ms; that is the documented boundary.
  - Truthful results: accepted requests are recorded as sent. Stale workers
    can't overwrite cancelled rows; their completion requires an in-flight row
    they still own.
  - Nothing stranded: delayed retries and retry-ready rows, and released or
    recovered rows, all end cancelled.
  - Completion and repeats: a cancelled campaign is never completed. Repeated
    cancel returns 409 invalid_state.
  - Limitation: cancellation cannot recall a request already on the wire.
    in_flight in the response is the upper bound on such requests.
